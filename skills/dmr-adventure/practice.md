# Adventure-design practice

What `dmr-adventure` designs by and reviews against. Two sources, kept apart:

- **The DMG**, the official guidance: read live, never from memory and never copied into a note.
- **Table practice**: three widely used principles, stated here in DM Realm's own words. They are advice, not rules: cite them by name ("the three-clue rule"), never as a book.

## The DMG

The Workspace's Edition decides the book: the 2024 *Dungeon Master's Guide* (`XDMG`) or the 2014 one (`DMG`). Read it through `dmr-trusted-source`, as a book's own text (`book/book-xdmg.json`, `book/book-dmg.json`), finding its sections by name, and cite book and page. Its chapter on creating adventures ("Chapter 4: Creating Adventures" in the 2024 book) is the backbone:

| Stage | Where the DMG speaks to it |
| --- | --- |
| Premise | Laying out the premise — the conflict, situations by level, the setting — and drawing the players in through patrons and hooks |
| Structure | Keeping the adventure moving, multiple ways to progress, and bringing it to an end |
| Content | Planning encounters — social, exploration and combat, monster behaviour, pace and tension — and the adventure's rewards |
| Review | All of the above, and the encounter-building rules for Difficulty |

**Encounter difficulty** is the encounter-building section of that chapter ("Combat Encounters" in the 2024 book: an XP budget per character by level and difficulty). Its numbers come from the book, every time.

Other DMG sections help where they fit: dungeons, traps, hazards, nonplayer characters, creating a creature or a magic item — each found and cited the same way.

## Table practice

### The three-clue rule

For every conclusion the party must reach to go on — who the culprit is, where the vault lies, that the bell is under the pier — the Adventure gives at least three clues, in different places. Players miss clues, misread them or ignore them; three gives the conclusion a fair chance. A Part's Clues section lists each conclusion and the notes that give it; Review counts them.

### Node-based design

An Adventure need not be a line of scenes. Its Parts, places and scenes are nodes, and the clues and leads are the paths between them: each node points to several others, so the party chooses its way and no single missed lead stops the game. A chain, a web or a hub with spokes are all shapes of it. A Part's Structure names its nodes and how they connect; its Ways out are the paths that leave it.

### The five-room dungeon

A short site, or a Part, built in five beats:

1. **An entrance with a guardian**: a reason the place is not already looted.
2. **A puzzle or a roleplaying challenge**: something fists cannot solve.
3. **A setback or a trick**: the place pushes back.
4. **The climax**: the big fight or the hard choice.
5. **A reward, or a revelation, or a twist**: what the party came for, or what changes the story.

The "rooms" are beats, not rooms: one can be a corridor, a conversation or a whole floor.
